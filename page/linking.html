Designator Linking Design

Each part on a board should be linked to the printed designator that names it (R12, C5, U3), or to nothing. This version records your one-to-one decision, explains B7 in detail with real numbers, shows where the best linker goes wrong, and walks through the transformer idea step by step. All examples are real WACV boards from the 42 test images, oracle track (annotated parts and annotated texts).

The task on a real board

1 · One-to-one

Decision (yours): a printed label names at most one part, and a part has at most one label. One label linked to two parts, or one part linked to two labels, is not allowed.

Both sides can still stay unmatched. A board does not pair every text with a part, or every part with a text. In WACV (45 images):

unmatched sidecountwhat they are
texts that name no annotated part1,222 (19%)R 295, C 122: mostly parts the annotators skipped (like the C12x capacitor). P 107, TP 53, PP 46: test points and pads. A, IO, GPIO, EM, FIL, MPD: pin or net names that happen to look like designators.
parts with no annotated label1,117 (17%)no label printed, label hidden under the part, or the annotation did not record it.

So the rule is one-to-one with a "none" option on the part side, and texts are allowed to stay unused. The "none" option is implemented as one extra column per part in the cost matrix:

Under strict one-to-one, the second box of a split part has to take "none" and is counted wrong. 90 of B8's 656 errors involve a shared designator, so a cleaned scoring (merging split boxes) would raise every method a little.

2 · How B7 works

B7 has three steps: build candidate pairs, describe each pair with 20 numbers, score each pair with a small tree model. Then Hungarian matching makes it one-to-one. It uses no neural network and no image pixels. The 20 numbers are computed with plain arithmetic from the two boxes (x1, y1, x2, y2) plus the length of the string.

Step 1 · Candidate pairs

For each part, rank every designator text on the board by centre distance ÷ part size. Keep the 12 nearest that are within 8 part sizes. Each kept (part, text) pair is one example. Its label is 1 if the text string equals the part's designator, else 0.

Per training fold that gives about 49,000 pairs, of which about 3,400 are true links (7%), from about 30 images. On the 42 test images the true label is among a part's candidates for 98% of parts. The other 2% (93 parts) can never be linked by B7 or B8.

Both labels are about one part length away (dist 1.09 vs 1.18), so distance alone barely separates them. The numbers that do are the competition features: C105 touches the part (gap 0) and no other part is closer to it (rank_t 0, n_closer 0), while three other parts are closer to the label C111 than this capacitor is (rank_t 3, n_closer 3).

Step 3 · The model, and how it is trained

The model is gradient-boosted decision trees (scikit-learn, 400 small trees of at most 31 leaves). Each tree asks yes/no questions about the 20 numbers, such as "gap < 0.1?" or "n_closer = 0?". The trees' answers add up to p(link). Training fits the trees so that true pairs get p near 1 and wrong pairs near 0. There is no feature extractor to train: the 20 numbers are fixed formulas, and only the trees are learned.

Then, per board, Hungarian matching on −log p, with the "none" cost set at p = 0.005, makes the result one-to-one. The boards are split into 3 folds, so every test image is scored by a model that never saw its board.

4 · The transformer, step by step

The idea in one sentence: give the model the whole board at once, so every part can see every label and every competing part before it decides. This is your point: assign and adjust jointly, instead of scoring each pair alone and fixing clashes at the end.

Below we use the same toy board as in section 1: three parts (P1, P2, P3) and three texts (C1, C2, TP1).

Step 1 · Turn every object into a token

A token is just a short list of numbers describing one object. One board becomes a set of tokens: one per part, one per text. Sizes and positions are measured in "part sizes" (the board's median part = 1), so a small photo and a large photo give the same numbers.

Step 3 · How position enters: 2D RoPE

Why not just put x and y into the token? Absolute position means nothing for linking. A label at (3000, 2000) next to a part at (2990, 1970) has the same relation as a label at (100, 50) next to a part at (90, 20). With raw coordinates the model would have to learn this from data for every place on the board. RoPE builds it in: the attention between two tokens depends only on their offset.

How, in plain words: take the token's query vector and group its numbers into pairs. Treat each pair as a clock hand and rotate it by an angle proportional to the token's x position (for half of the pairs) or y position (for the other half). Do the same to the key vector of the other token. The match between the two (their dot product) then depends only on the difference of the two angles, that is, on the offset between the tokens. Different pairs rotate at different speeds, like rulers of different lengths: some notice a shift of one part size, others a shift of ten.

q̃a = R(ω·xa) qa,   k̃b = R(ω·xb) kb  ⇒   q̃aT k̃b = qaT R(ω·(xb − xa)) kb
2D: half of the pairs rotate with x, half with y; pair k has its own frequency ωk.

The string, split in two

"C105" → prefix "C" (a learned embedding per prefix: R, C, L, D, Q, U, FB, SW, …) and number 105 (embeddings of its digits plus its value). The prefix says "this should be a capacitor". The number may carry order, since parts in a row often have consecutive numbers (C104, C105, C106). In the DigitalDiscovery label tables the numbers are the only link between the grid of labels and the grid of parts. Whether the order helps is a hypothesis that E2 vs E1 will test.

The image, one crop per token

Each crop is resized (e.g. 64 × 64) and passed through one small CNN shared by parts and texts. The result goes into the token's "image crop" slot.
